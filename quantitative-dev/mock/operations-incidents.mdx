Interviewers want to know whether you can keep a trading or data system healthy and respond calmly when it is not. This page has nine operations and incident questions with follow-ups and answer points. The answers are frameworks, and specific compliance and reporting duties follow each firm's and regulator's rules.

<Info>
  **Status of this page.** Last checked on 15 November 2026. These are general practices, not any firm's incident process.
</Info>

## A framework for incident questions

1. **Stop the bleeding:** Limit further damage first (pause, kill switch, fall back to a backup source).
2. **Assess impact:** Who and what is affected, and by how much? Notify risk, compliance and managers as procedures require.
3. **Diagnose:** Find the root cause with evidence from logs, metrics and recorded data.
4. **Fix and verify:** Apply the fix, verify it, then resume carefully.
5. **Review:** Write a blameless postmortem with a timeline, root cause, detection gap and follow-up actions.

## Data and batch jobs

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Q1. A container running a nightly job is killed with no error message. How do you investigate?">
    A common cause is running out of memory (an OOM kill).

    - Check the container's exit status and reason, and the host's system logs for OOM records.
    - Check the container's memory and CPU limits.
    - Compare data volume with earlier runs, or look for recent code changes.
    - Switch to chunked processing or adjust the resource settings.

    **Prevention:** Add memory monitoring and failure alerts to the job so there are no silent failures. See [Data engineering](/quantitative-research/interview/data-engineering) Q9 and Q10.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Q2. A daily data pipeline silently skipped yesterday and nobody noticed until a researcher complained. How do you prevent a repeat?">
    - **Freshness monitoring:** Alert when expected data for a trading day has not arrived by a set time, using the trading calendar to know what is expected.
    - **Row count and value checks:** Compare each day's volume of data with recent history and flag large deviations.
    - **Job-level alerts:** Alert on failure and also on jobs that never started.
    - **Safe re-runs:** Make the pipeline idempotent so a backfill for the missing day is safe. See [Data engineering](/quantitative-research/interview/data-engineering) Q8.
    - **Ownership:** Each dataset has a named owner and a documented recovery runbook.

    The principle is that silence must never be treated as success.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## Live connectivity and data

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Q3. You detect a gap in the sequence numbers of a market data feed. What do you do?">
    - **Detect:** Track the expected next sequence number per channel and flag any jump.
    - **Contain:** Mark affected books as uncertain so strategies do not trade on a possibly wrong book. Widen quotes or pause for those instruments.
    - **Recover:** Use the redundant feed if you have one, request retransmission, or fetch a snapshot and rebuild the book from the incremental updates that follow it.
    - **Verify and resume:** Check the rebuilt book for sanity (for example it is not crossed) before trading again.
    - **Record:** Log the gap for later analysis, because frequent gaps point to a network or capacity problem.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Q4. Your connection to an exchange or broker drops in the middle of the day. Walk me through the recovery.">
    - **Know your state:** The system keeps its own sent and received sequence numbers and a durable log of all messages.
    - **Reconnect:** Use the backup connection if one is ready, otherwise reconnect and log on again.
    - **Gap fill:** Compare sequence numbers with the counterparty and request resends for messages you missed. Handle messages the counterparty did not receive.
    - **Reconcile orders and positions:** After reconnecting, query open orders and positions from the venue and compare them with your records. Treat the venue as the authority.
    - **Do not blindly resend:** Orders in an unknown state must be checked, because resending can create duplicates.
    - **Safety:** While disconnected, stop generating new orders, and consider whether open orders should be cancelled on disconnect.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Q5. A wrong price was published to clients for 10 minutes. Walk me through your response.">
    **Order: stop the bleeding, assess, fix, review.**

    - **Stop the bleeding:** Use the kill switch or fall back to a backup source immediately to stop publishing the bad price.
    - **Assess:** Determine which clients, trades and positions were affected and by how much, and notify risk, compliance and the relevant managers.
    - **Fix:** Find the root cause (data, configuration or a code change), and verify the fix before resuming.
    - **Review:** Write a blameless postmortem with the timeline, root cause, why it was not detected sooner and follow-up actions (automated checks, limits, tests).

    Specific compliance and reporting obligations follow the firm's and regulators' rules. In the interview, say that you would escalate according to company procedure.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Q6. A strategy suddenly sends orders at ten times its normal rate. What do you do in the first five minutes?">
    - **Stop it first:** trigger the strategy's kill switch or cancel-on-disconnect, and block new orders for that strategy. Do not wait to understand the cause.
    - **Check the damage:** open orders, fills and positions created during the spike, and whether any limit was breached. Cancel what is still open.
    - **Contain:** check whether other strategies share the same code, configuration or data feed, and pause them if they might be affected.
    - **Escalate:** inform the trading desk, risk and compliance according to procedure, since venues may also contact you about message rates.
    - **Diagnose afterwards:** typical causes are a feedback loop (reacting to its own orders), a bad price or a stale book, a configuration error, or a retry loop after rejects.

    **Follow-up:** What control would have stopped it automatically?

    → An order and message rate limit in the pre-trade risk layer that is independent of the strategy, with automatic disable on breach. See [System design](/quantitative-dev/mock/system-design) Q8 and Q9.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Q7. Timestamps from two servers disagree by several milliseconds. Why does it matter and how do you fix it?">
    **Why it matters:** event ordering across machines, latency measurement, as-of joins in research, and regulatory record keeping all assume clocks agree. Some regulators, such as the EU under MiFID II, set maximum allowed divergence from UTC for trading timestamps.

    **Fixes:**

    - Synchronize with a proper time source. NTP is usually accurate to milliseconds, while PTP with hardware timestamping can reach microseconds or better.
    - Monitor the offset of every host from the reference and alert when it drifts beyond a threshold.
    - Record which clock produced each timestamp, and keep the exchange's timestamp next to your receive time.
    - For ordering within one process, use a monotonic clock, not the wall clock, which can jump when it is corrected.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## Change management

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Q8. How do you deploy a change to a live pricing or trading system safely?">
    - **Pre-release:** Tests including replay of recorded sessions, and review of the change and its blast radius.
    - **Shadow mode:** Run the new version on live data without sending orders, and compare its decisions with the current version.
    - **Staged rollout:** Enable for a small set of instruments or a small size first, with tight limits and close monitoring.
    - **Feature flags:** Turn the new behavior on and off without redeploying.
    - **Consistency checks:** Verify that every instance runs the same version and configuration, because partial deployments are a classic cause of incidents.
    - **Rollback:** A rehearsed, fast path back to the previous version, with clear criteria for when to use it.
    - **Timing:** Avoid risky changes at market open, close and around major events.

    Public interview guides frequently cite Knight Capital's 2012 loss when motivating deployment controls and kill switches.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Q9. How do you write a good postmortem?">
    - **Blameless:** Focus on how the system and process allowed the failure, not on who made the mistake.
    - **Timeline:** What happened, when it was detected, when it was mitigated and when it was resolved.
    - **Impact:** Clients, trades, positions and money affected, stated factually.
    - **Root cause and contributing factors:** Distinguish the trigger from the underlying weaknesses (missing checks, unclear ownership, weak monitoring).
    - **Detection gap:** Why it was not caught earlier, and which alert would have caught it.
    - **Actions:** Specific, owned, time-bound follow-ups such as new automated checks, tighter limits, extra tests or runbook updates, tracked to completion.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>
