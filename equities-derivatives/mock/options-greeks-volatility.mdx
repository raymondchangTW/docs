This page covers the options questions that equity derivatives desks ask most often: Greeks, parity, replication, volatility and hedging. Interviewers use them to see whether you can reason about a position, not whether you can recite a formula, so explain the intuition and the P&L consequence in each answer. Formulas are given in words where possible.

<Info>
  **Status of this page.** Last checked on 15 November 2026. These are general practice frameworks, not any firm's interview content. Worked numbers are illustrative.
</Info>

## Greeks

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Q1. What happens to delta and gamma as the stock price rises? Why is an at-the-money call delta slightly above 0.5?">
    - **Call delta** rises from near 0 (deep out of the money) toward 1 (deep in the money) as the stock rises. Put delta moves from near minus 1 toward 0.
    - **Gamma** is highest near the money and shortest-dated, and falls toward zero deep in or out of the money. It is positive for both calls and puts when you are long the option.
    - **At-the-money delta** is slightly above 0.5 for a call because the delta of a call is the normal distribution function of d1, and d1 is positive at the money: it contains the drift term from interest rates and the half-variance term from volatility. With a continuous dividend yield the call delta is the dividend discount factor times that value, so for a high-dividend stock the at-the-money call delta can be **below** 0.5. State the assumption you are using.

    **Follow-up:** What does this mean for a short-dated at-the-money option near expiry?

    → Gamma becomes very large, so delta flips quickly between near 0 and near 1 for small stock moves. This is why near-expiry at-the-money positions are the hardest to hedge.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Q2. Explain the link between gamma and theta. When does a delta-hedged long option position make money?">
    **Gamma and theta are two sides of the same trade.** A long option position has positive gamma and pays negative theta (time decay). A short option position has the opposite.

    For a delta-hedged position, the approximate result over a short period is that you gain from gamma when the stock moves more than the market implied and lose the theta you paid. In words: you make money when the realized volatility is higher than the implied volatility you paid for, and lose when it is lower.

    **Follow-up:** Why is it not exactly that simple in practice?

    → Hedging is discrete and has costs, realized volatility can come in jumps rather than a smooth path, and the implied volatility itself changes, which brings in vega. The result is a P&L that depends on the path, not only the average realized volatility.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Q3. How often should you delta hedge?">
    It is a trade-off between **hedging error** and **transaction cost**. Hedging more often reduces the error from gamma between hedges but pays the spread and market impact each time.

    Practical approaches rebalance when delta moves outside a band, or at set times, rather than continuously. The band widens when costs are high or gamma is low, and narrows when gamma is large. Say that the right choice depends on the stock's liquidity, the size of gamma and the desk's risk tolerance.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## Parity and replication

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Q4. State put-call parity and use it to replicate a forward with two vanilla options.">
    For European options with the same strike and expiry: the call price minus the put price equals the stock price minus the present value of the strike and, if there are dividends, minus the present value of the expected dividends.

    **Replication:** a long call and a short put with the same strike and expiry behave like a long forward at that strike. At expiry the payoff is the stock price minus the strike in every scenario.

    **Follow-up:** What do you do if parity is violated?

    → Check inputs first (dividends, borrow cost, rates, American versus European exercise). If a real gap remains, sell the rich side and buy the cheap side, with the stock or a forward as the hedge, and account for borrow and financing costs.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Q5. How would you hedge a digital (binary) option?">
    A digital option pays a fixed amount if the stock is above the strike at expiry. It is approximated by a **tight call spread**: buy a call at the strike and sell a call at a slightly higher strike, scaled so the maximum payoff equals the digital payout.

    **Why this matters for the desk:**

    - Near the strike close to expiry, delta spikes and gamma becomes very large and flips from positive to negative across the strike, so the hedge can swing between buying and selling on small moves. This is called pin risk.
    - Traders often hedge with a slightly wider call spread, which behaves like a smoother payoff, and so the product is priced with a margin to cover this.
    - The digital's value is linked to the volatility skew, because the call spread depends on the slope of the smile at the strike.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## Volatility

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Q6. What is volatility skew, and why do equity index puts usually trade at higher implied volatility than calls?">
    Implied volatility differs by strike. For equity indices, **lower strikes usually have higher implied volatility**, so the curve slopes downward. The usual reasons given:

    - Demand for downside protection from institutions, which pushes up put prices.
    - Equity falls tend to come with volatility rising, and sell-offs are sharper than rallies.
    - The Black-Scholes assumptions (constant volatility and no jumps) do not hold, and the market prices that.

    **Follow-up:** What does skew mean for a risk reversal?

    → A risk reversal (long call, short put, or the reverse) is priced largely off the skew. When skew is steep, selling downside puts earns a premium, but it concentrates the crash risk.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Q7. A stock reports earnings tomorrow and the at-the-money straddle costs 6% of the stock price. What does the market expect, and how do you judge whether it is cheap?">
    A short-dated at-the-money straddle costs roughly 0.8 times the stock price times the volatility times the square root of time. So its price divided by the stock price is a rough estimate of the **expected absolute move**, here about 6%.

    **To judge it:** compare 6% with the stock's typical earnings-day moves over past quarters, adjusted for the current environment. If past moves have averaged well above 6%, the straddle looks cheap, and if below, it looks rich. Remember that the straddle also contains normal non-event volatility for the days left.

    **Follow-up:** After the announcement, what happens to implied volatility?

    → It usually drops sharply because the uncertainty is resolved (volatility crush). A long option position can lose on vega even if the stock moves, unless the move is larger than the price implied.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Q8. Compare a calendar spread with a butterfly. What are the Greeks of a calendar spread?">
    - **Calendar spread:** sell a near-dated option and buy a longer-dated option at the same strike. At the money it is **long vega, positive theta and slightly short gamma**, because the near-dated option decays faster and has higher gamma. It profits if the stock stays near the strike and if longer-dated implied volatility rises.
    - **Butterfly:** buy one low strike, sell two middle strikes and buy one high strike. It has a limited loss (the premium) and a limited gain concentrated around the middle strike. It is a bet on the stock finishing near the middle, and is short volatility around that point.

    State the maximum loss and maximum gain of each when you answer, because interviewers often ask for them.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## Quick estimates and advanced follow-ups

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Q9. Without a calculator, roughly what is a 3-month at-the-money call worth on a 100 stock with 20% volatility?">
    With low rates and no dividends, an at-the-money call is worth about **0.4 times the stock price times the volatility times the square root of time**. Here that is 0.4 times 100 times 0.2 times 0.5, or about **4.0**. The at-the-money straddle is about twice that, which is consistent with the 0.8 rule in Q7.

    **Follow-up:** Is delta the probability that the option expires in the money?

    → No. Call delta is the normal distribution function of d1, while the risk-neutral probability of finishing in the money is the normal distribution function of d2, which is smaller. The gap grows with volatility and time, so for long-dated or high-volatility options the two numbers can differ a lot.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Q10. When would you exercise an American call early?">
    On a non-dividend stock, never: the call is worth more alive than its intrinsic value, because you keep the time value and delay paying the strike.

    On a dividend stock, early exercise can only be optimal **just before an ex-dividend date**. Exercise when the dividend you capture is larger than what you give up: the remaining time value of the option plus the interest saved by paying the strike later. A rough first check is whether the dividend exceeds the strike times the interest rate times the time from the ex-date to expiry. If it does not, early exercise just before that ex-date is not optimal.

    **Follow-up:** You are short deep in-the-money calls before an ex-date. What is your risk?

    → Assignment. If holders exercise, you deliver the stock and lose the dividend your hedge would have received. Desks track this as dividend or early-exercise risk around ex-dates.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Q11. How do you replicate a variance swap, and why is the variance strike usually above at-the-money implied volatility?">
    A variance swap pays realized variance minus a fixed strike. It is replicated by a **static strip of out-of-the-money puts and calls across all strikes, weighted by one over the strike squared**, plus a dynamic delta hedge. The fair strike comes from option prices directly, without a volatility model, which is also how the VIX is built.

    - **Above at-the-money volatility:** the 1 over strike squared weighting puts large weight on low-strike puts. With a downward-sloping equity skew those puts carry high implied volatility, so the variance strike sits above at-the-money volatility.
    - **Volatility swap versus variance swap:** because the square root is concave, the fair volatility swap strike is below the square root of the variance strike. The difference is a convexity adjustment, and it grows with the volatility of volatility.

    **Follow-up:** Why do dealers cap variance swaps on single stocks?

    → A crash makes realized variance explode, and the strip cannot be built in practice at very low strikes where options do not trade. Caps limit that tail.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Q12. What are sticky strike and sticky delta, and how do they change your hedge?">
    They are assumptions about how the volatility smile moves when the stock moves.

    - **Sticky strike:** each strike keeps its implied volatility. With a downward skew, at-the-money volatility rises as the stock falls, because a lower strike becomes at the money.
    - **Sticky delta (sticky moneyness):** the smile moves with the stock, so at-the-money volatility stays the same.
    - **Local volatility dynamics:** the implied volatility of a fixed strike moves in the direction of the skew slope, so with a downward skew it falls when the stock rises.

    **Hedge impact:** the true hedge ratio is the Black-Scholes delta plus vega times the change in that option's implied volatility per unit move in the stock. Under sticky strike the adjustment is zero. With a downward skew, sticky moneyness raises a call's hedge ratio and local volatility lowers it. Say which regime the desk assumes and check it against how the smile actually moved in past sell-offs.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>
