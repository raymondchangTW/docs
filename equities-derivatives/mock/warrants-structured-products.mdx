This page covers warrant and structured product questions. Interviewers use them to test whether you understand the issuer's side: what risks the desk holds after selling a product, and how the product is built from simple options. For issuance and liquidity rules in Hong Kong, use the linked pages.

<Info>
  **Status of this page.** Last checked on 15 November 2026. These are general practice frameworks, not any firm's interview content. Numbers in examples are illustrative, not market quotes.
</Info>

## Warrants and CBBCs

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title={"Q1. You issued call warrants and the stock jumps 5%. What happens to your hedge and your P&L?"}>
    Issuing call warrants is the same as being short an option, so you are **short gamma**.

    - When the stock rises, the option's delta increases and your hedge is too small, so you need to **buy more stock** at the higher price.
    - That is the cost of short gamma: buying high and selling low. The approximate loss is half the gamma times the square of the dollar move.
    - Theta income, together with the premium collected, offsets part of this loss. Overall you lose when realized volatility is above the implied volatility used in pricing.

    **Follow-up:** What other risks do you have besides delta?

    → Vega (changes in implied volatility), dividends, interest rates, and jump risk across the gap. For issuance and liquidity rules, see [Warrants issuance](/equities-derivatives/hk/warrants/issuance) and [liquidity provider rules](/equities-derivatives/hk/warrants/liquidity-provider-rules).
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Q2. How is the hedge size for a warrant calculated?">
    In Hong Kong the entitlement ratio is the **number of warrants needed for one share** (for example 10 to 1). So the hedge in shares equals the option delta, times the number of warrants held by investors, **divided by** the entitlement ratio. For a call you hold that many shares, and for a put you short them.

    **Example:** 50 million call warrants held by investors, entitlement ratio 10, delta 0.4. The hedge is 0.4 times 50 million divided by 10, or **2 million shares**.

    Use the warrants held by investors, not the total issued: warrants still in the issuer's own inventory (from issuance or bought back as liquidity provider) are not a liability to hedge.

    The hedge is adjusted as delta changes with the stock price, time and volatility. Say that the desk also manages gamma and vega across all its warrants on the same underlying, not warrant by warrant.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Q3. Where does an issuer make or lose money on warrants?">
    **Sources of profit:** the margin between the price the issuer sells at and its hedging cost (including a volatility margin), the bid-ask spread when it acts as liquidity provider, and time decay on options that expire out of the money.

    **Sources of loss:** realized volatility above implied, gaps and jumps across hedge points, a stock halt where the hedge cannot be adjusted, hedging slippage, and changes in implied volatility that move the book against the position.

    See [Warrants trading rules](/equities-derivatives/hk/warrants/trading-rules) for the market context.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Q4. How does the issuer's risk on a bull CBBC differ from that on a call warrant?">
    A callable bull contract has a **call level**. If the underlying touches it, the product is called and trading stops, so it is a knock-out structure.

    - Far from the call level, the CBBC behaves almost like a leveraged long position, so the hedge is close to one share per entitlement unit, with little gamma.
    - Near the call level, the risk is the **gap through the barrier** and the unwind of the hedge at a worse price than the call level. Some categories also carry a residual value after a call, which the issuer must handle.

    Details of categories and settlement are on the [CBBC page](/equities-derivatives/hk/warrants/cbbc).

    **Follow-up:** A Category R bull CBBC is called. How is the residual value set, and what does the issuer do with its hedge?

    → After a mandatory call event, the valuation period runs from the call until the end of the following trading session. For a bull contract, the residual value is the lowest spot price in that period minus the strike, divided by the entitlement ratio, and zero if that is negative. Category N contracts, where the call price equals the strike, pay nothing. The issuer unwinds its long hedge during the valuation period. If it sells above the lowest price, it keeps the difference, and if the stock gaps below the strike, it bears the loss. That is why the gap between call price and strike is part of the issuer's pricing.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## Structured products

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Q5. How is a bonus certificate constructed?">
    A bonus certificate pays a **bonus level** at maturity if the underlying never touches a barrier below, and otherwise tracks the underlying.

    **Building blocks:** the underlying (or a zero-strike call) plus a **down-and-out put** with strike at the bonus level and barrier at the protection level. If the barrier is never touched, the put pays the difference up to the bonus level. If the barrier is touched, the put disappears and the holder keeps only the underlying.

    **Follow-up:** What is the main risk for the issuer?

    → Behavior near the barrier: the put's value and delta change sharply around it, and the hedge is difficult to manage there.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Q6. Build a capital-protected note with participation in an index. How do you find the participation rate?">
    A capital-protected note is a **zero-coupon bond plus a call option**. The bond pays the protected amount at maturity, and the money left over after buying the bond pays for the call.

    **Illustration (not market quotes):** with a 3-year rate of 4%, a bond paying 100 at maturity costs about 88.9 today. That leaves about 11.1 per 100 invested to buy options. If an at-the-money 3-year call costs 15, the participation rate is about 11.1 divided by 15, or roughly 74%.

    **What changes it:** higher rates leave more budget, higher implied volatility makes the call more expensive and reduces the participation, and the issuer's fees and funding reduce it further.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Q7. In an autocallable or reverse convertible, which option is the client short, and what does the dealer hold?">
    The client earns a coupon in exchange for selling a **down-and-in put** on the underlying (or the worst of a basket). The coupon is funded by the premium of that put. If the barrier is breached and the final level is below the strike, the client takes the loss.

    **The dealer therefore holds that put**, and the main risks to name are:

    - **Skew:** the put is on a low strike, so its value depends on the volatility smile.
    - **Barrier and observation dates:** delta and gamma can change sharply near the barrier and on autocall dates.
    - **Correlation for worst-of products:** the dealer holds a put on the worst performer, so lower correlation makes that put more valuable. The dealer's position is sensitive to correlation, and it cannot be hedged cleanly with simple instruments.
    - **Dividends**, which lower the forward and raise the put value.

    **Follow-up:** How would you pitch a structured product to a client?

    → Start from the client's view and risk appetite, show the payoff in best, middle and worst cases, explain what they give up in return (here, the downside from the put), state the risks and costs plainly, and confirm suitability.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Q8. What is an accumulator, which side is the client on, and what went wrong in 2008?">
    The client agrees to **buy a fixed number of shares at each fixing at a strike below the current price**. If the stock rises above a knock-out level, the contract ends. If the stock falls below the strike, the client usually has to buy a multiple (often twice) the normal quantity at the strike.

    **Building blocks per fixing:** a long call and a short put at the strike, with the put quantity geared up (often twice), all with an up-and-out barrier. The client is long the capped upside and **short geared puts**. The dealer holds the opposite: long the puts and short the knocked-out calls.

    **Risks to name:**

    - **Client:** small, capped gains in rising markets and large, geared losses in falling markets. In the 2008 sell-off losses were large enough that the product picked up the nickname "I kill you later" in Hong Kong.
    - **Dealer:** client credit and margin risk when the stock falls (the client may not pay), gap and barrier risk near the knock-out, and concentration if many clients hold the same name.

    **Follow-up:** Why is the strike set below spot at inception?

    → Because the client is selling geared puts. Their premium pays for the discount, so the product costs nothing up front.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## Warrant market making

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Q9. A stock is at 100. A call warrant costs 0.20, the entitlement ratio is 10 and delta is 0.5. What are the gearing and the effective gearing?">
    - **Gearing** is the stock price divided by the cost of controlling one share through warrants: 100 divided by (0.20 times 10), or **50 times**.
    - **Effective gearing** is gearing times delta: 50 times 0.5, or **25 times**. A 1% move in the stock moves the warrant by about 25%.

    Effective gearing is the better measure of sensitivity, but it changes with the stock price, time and implied volatility, so treat it as a snapshot. The entitlement ratio changes the price per warrant but not the effective gearing.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Q10. Why can the implied volatility of a warrant differ from that of the listed option on the same stock, and how does the issuer manage it?">
    A warrant is a retail product sold by a single issuer acting as liquidity provider, while the listed option trades in a competitive market. Differences come from:

    - **Issuer margin:** the issuer prices in its hedging costs and profit.
    - **Supply and demand:** retail investors mostly buy calls and puts, so issuers are structurally short options and need a margin for short gamma and short vega.
    - **Competition between issuers** on the same underlying, which compresses the margin.

    **Follow-up:** Why do issuers try to keep warrant implied volatility stable?

    → Investors watch it. If the issuer lowers implied volatility after investors buy, warrant prices fall even when the stock does not move, which damages the issuer's reputation and future flow. Issuers therefore manage the book's vega with listed options or other issuance rather than by moving the warrant price.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>
