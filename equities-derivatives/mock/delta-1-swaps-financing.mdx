This page covers Delta-1 questions: swaps, financing, borrow, dividends and counterparty risk. The interviewer wants to see that you understand who holds which exposure when a client trades, because that decides the hedge and the pricing. For the structure of these products on this site, see the linked pages.

<Info>
  **Status of this page.** Last checked on 15 November 2026. These are general practice frameworks, not any firm's interview content or desk practice. Contract terms, tax treatment and margin rules depend on the jurisdiction and the documents, so say that you would check them.
</Info>

## Products

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Q1. What is a total return swap, and how is it structured?">
    A total return swap (TRS) lets one party receive the **total return of an underlying** (price change plus dividends or other income) without owning it, in exchange for paying a **funding leg**, usually a reference rate plus a spread. The other party receives the funding payment and pays the return.

    - The client gets economic exposure, and the dealer usually hedges by holding the underlying (client long) or a borrowed short (client short).
    - Payments are periodic, with resets, and the contract sets how dividends, corporate actions and termination are handled.
    - It can be written on a single stock, a basket or an index.

    **Follow-up:** Why do clients use swaps instead of buying the stock?

    → Leverage through margin, balance sheet efficiency, access to markets or shorting that is hard to get directly, and in some cases confidentiality or tax treatment. Each reason depends on the client and the jurisdiction. See [OTC derivatives](/equities-derivatives/global/delta-1-otc).
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Q2. What is the difference between a CFD and an equity swap?">
    Both give synthetic exposure to a stock, and both are OTC. The usual distinctions:

    - **Documentation and client type:** CFDs are typically offered by brokers to a wide range of clients on standard terms with margin and daily financing, while equity swaps are usually institutional contracts under master agreements with negotiated terms.
    - **Term and reset:** An equity swap often has a fixed term and periodic resets. A CFD is usually open ended.
    - **Tax and regulation:** Treatment of stamp duty, withholding tax and leverage limits differs by country and has changed over time.

    Do not claim a fixed rule. Say that the economics are similar and that the differences come mainly from documentation, client segment and local tax and regulation.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## Pricing and hedging

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Q3. How does the hedge of a swap differ if the client is long versus short?">
    - **Client long:** The dealer buys the stock to hedge, which needs funding, and the client pays the funding cost.
    - **Client short:** The dealer needs a short hedge, which generates cash and requires borrowing the stock. The client pays the borrow fee and receives the interest on cash net of costs.

    Both sides have dividend and tax effects. Be clear that the client's direction decides which funding and borrow exposure the dealer holds.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Q4. A client wants to go short a hard-to-borrow stock via a total return swap. How do you price it?">
    When the client is short through the swap, the dealer is long the stock through the same swap, so it hedges by **borrowing the stock and selling it short**. The price needs to reflect:

    - The funding spread on the hedge and the capital usage
    - **Borrow cost**, which is typically high for hard-to-borrow names and can change
    - Dividend handling and tax
    - The risk that the stock loan is recalled

    **Follow-up:** What if the lender recalls the stock?

    → First look for another borrow source. If none is available, you need a matching clause in the contract (for example early termination or a fee adjustment), otherwise the dealer carries the gap. The specific terms depend on the contract.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Q5. A client buys a 100 million swap on a basket of stocks. How do you hedge it and what risks remain?">
    **Hedge:** buy the basket, or buy futures or an ETF if the basket is large or illiquid, and fund the position.

    **Residual risks:**

    - Basis between the futures or ETF and the actual basket, if you hedge with a proxy.
    - Dividends and withholding tax differences between what the swap pays and what the hedge receives.
    - Funding spread changes and borrow availability.
    - Corporate actions and index changes that the swap terms handle differently from the hedge.
    - Execution cost when building and unwinding the hedge, especially for illiquid names.

    See [Delta-1: hedging with listed derivatives](/equities-derivatives/global/delta-1-hedging).
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Q6. How do dividends and withholding tax affect a swap?">
    The swap usually passes the dividend to the client, sometimes at a rate that reflects withholding tax. The dealer's hedge receives the actual dividend after the tax that applies to the dealer. If those two amounts differ, the difference is a **P&L item for the dealer**.

    Pricing therefore includes an expected dividend and a tax assumption. Say that tax treatment depends on the underlying market, the client's status and the documents, and that you would confirm it with the tax and legal teams.

    **Follow-up:** A non-US client wants a swap on a US stock. What changes?

    → Under US section 871(m), dividend equivalents on delta-one swaps referencing US equities are treated as US-source dividends and subject to withholding. The rules for non-delta-one transactions have been repeatedly phased in later: the latest IRS notice (Notice 2026-61, September 2026) keeps non-delta-one transactions issued through 2028 outside the rules. So the dividend the swap passes through is net of US withholding, and the dealer's systems must identify which trades are in scope.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## Operations and risk

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Q7. What happens to a swap when the underlying has a corporate action such as a rights issue, spin-off or suspension?">
    The contract defines how the position is adjusted, usually through a calculation agent applying standard equity derivative terms or the specific confirmation. The hedge is adjusted in parallel, for example by taking part in the rights issue or receiving the spin-off shares.

    **Points to name:** differences between the contract adjustment and what the hedge actually receives, a suspended stock that cannot be traded or valued, and the possible early termination terms. The details depend on the documents.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Q8. A client builds a large, concentrated leveraged position through swaps with several dealers. What are the risks for you?">
    - **Concentration:** The dealer holds a large hedge in a few stocks, and a client default would mean selling it into the market.
    - **Gap risk:** A fall in the stock can exceed the margin before it is called.
    - **Hidden aggregate exposure:** Each dealer sees only its own swaps, so the client's total leverage may be much higher than any single dealer sees.

    **Controls:** initial and variation margin sized for the liquidity and concentration of the position, concentration limits, haircuts for illiquid names, daily margining, and a plan for how the hedge would be unwound. The 2021 default of a single family office (Archegos) with swaps at several banks is the case interviewers most often cite.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## Futures, dividends and index events

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Q9. A stock index trades at 100, its 3-month future at 101, and the present value of dividends to expiry is 0.5. What financing rate does the future imply, and what do you do with it?">
    The future is approximately the spot minus the dividends, grown at the financing rate: future equals (spot minus PV of dividends) times (1 plus rate times time). So the implied rate is (101 divided by 99.5, minus 1) divided by 0.25, or about **6.0% annualized**.

    **Use it:** compare the implied rate with your own funding cost.

    - If the implied rate is above your funding cost, the future is rich: buy the basket, fund it, and sell the future (cash and carry).
    - If it is below, the future is cheap, and a client who wants long exposure is better served by futures than a swap, or a desk long the basket can sell it and buy futures to release balance sheet.

    **Follow-up:** Why does the implied rate jump around quarter-end and year-end?

    → Banks' balance sheet costs rise at reporting dates, so the cost of holding the basket rises and the futures roll prices it in. Roll periods, when positions move from the front contract to the next, show the market's view of financing for the next quarter.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Q10. Why do dividend futures and dividend swaps exist, and who is on each side?">
    They let you trade the **dividends paid over a period** separately from the price of the index.

    - **Natural sellers:** dealers who issue structured products such as autocallables are left long dividend exposure through their hedges (Q7 on the structured products page), and they sell dividend futures or swaps to reduce it.
    - **Natural buyers:** investors who want income exposure or a view that payouts will be higher than priced.
    - **Delta-1 link:** the expected dividend is an input into every futures and swap price, so the desk marks its dividend assumption against the dividend futures curve.

    **Follow-up:** What is the main risk of a long dividend position?

    → Payout cuts in a crisis, which happened widely in 2020, and regulatory pressure on banks to suspend dividends. Dividend futures fall sharply in these episodes, more than the index in percentage terms for the near years.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Q11. A stock will be added to a major index at the close next week. A client asks you to guarantee the closing price for a large buy. How do you think about it?">
    You are being asked to take the risk that the closing auction prints away from where you can execute.

    - **Flows:** index funds must buy at the close on the effective date, so demand is predictable and other participants position ahead of it. The stock often rises before the event and the close can be crowded.
    - **Execution:** you can pre-hedge by buying before the close, which is a risk position that loses if the price falls back, or execute in the auction alongside the index demand, which risks a large imbalance.
    - **Pricing:** charge for the expected impact and the variance of the close versus your execution, and set a size limit.
    - **Conduct:** pre-hedging must follow the firm's rules and the client's agreement, and you must not trade ahead of the client in a way that harms them.

    **Follow-up:** What happens on the stock being removed from the index?

    → The reverse: forced selling at the close. Short sellers and swap desks can face borrow recalls, because index funds that lent the stock sell it.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>
