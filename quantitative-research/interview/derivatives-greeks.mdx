This page collects equity derivatives questions for quantitative interviews, covering option pricing, Greeks, the volatility surface and forward pricing.

## Pricing relationships

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Q1. State put-call parity. What do you do if it is violated?">
    For European options with no dividends: `C − P = S − K e^(−rT)`.

    - Continuous dividend yield q: `C − P = S e^(−qT) − K e^(−rT)`
    - Discrete dividends: replace S with `S − PV(dividends)`

    If it fails, you can lock in a riskless profit with a conversion or reversal. For example, if `C − P` is above the right-hand side, sell the call, buy the put, buy the stock and borrow cash. In practice, borrow cost, funding rates and bid-ask spreads mean parity holds as a band rather than an equality. Conversely, you can back out the implied borrow cost or implied dividend from call and put prices.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Q2. What is the fair forward or futures price of a stock or index? When does cash-and-carry arbitrage work?">
    With a continuous dividend yield: `F = S e^((r − q) T)`. With discrete dividends: `F = (S − PV(D)) e^(rT)`.

    If F is above this value: buy the spot, sell the future and deliver at expiry (cash-and-carry). If F is below: short the spot and buy the future (reverse cash-and-carry). The basis, F minus S, comes from financing cost net of expected dividends and converges to zero at expiry.

    In practice, borrow cost, funding spreads, dividend uncertainty and margin all widen the no-arbitrage band. This is also the basis for Delta-1 arbitrage between index futures and ETFs. See [Delta-1: hedging with listed derivatives](/equities-derivatives/global/delta-1-hedging).
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Q3. Explain risk-neutral pricing. Why does the real-world drift not appear in the Black-Scholes price?">
    An option can be replicated with the underlying and cash, and the replication cost is the no-arbitrage price. The replication strategy does not depend on your view of the stock's drift μ, so μ is replaced by the risk-free rate r in the price.

    Under the risk-neutral measure Q, price equals the discounted expected payoff: `V = e^(−rT) E_Q[payoff]`. This does not mean market participants are risk neutral. It is a computational device. Volatility σ stays in the formula because the replication cost depends on how much the path moves.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Q4. Should you ever exercise an American call early? An American put?">
    **Call, no dividends:** Never exercise early. Doing so gives up time value and the interest on the strike, and the call is worth more than its intrinsic value.

    **Call, with dividends:** It can be optimal to exercise just before an ex-dividend date, when the dividend exceeds the remaining time value plus the interest earned on the strike.

    **Put:** Early exercise can be optimal, especially when deep in the money and rates are high, because receiving the strike early earns interest while the remaining time value is small.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Q5. How do dividends affect call and put prices?">
    Higher expected dividends **lower call prices and raise put prices**, because the stock drops by the dividend on the ex-date and the option holder does not receive it. This also shows up as a lower forward price, and it is why S is replaced by `S e^(−qT)` in put-call parity.

    For trading: dividend expectations and dividend risk are separate risk factors, and they remain after delta hedging. See the Dividends row in [Delta-1: hedging with listed derivatives](/equities-derivatives/global/delta-1-hedging).
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## Black-Scholes and its limits

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Q6. List the Black-Scholes assumptions. Which fail most badly in practice?">
    **Assumptions:** The stock follows geometric Brownian motion (lognormal) with constant drift and volatility, a constant risk-free rate, continuous trading, no transaction costs, unlimited short selling and no arbitrage.

    **Most commonly violated:**

    - **Constant volatility:** Real markets show a volatility smile or skew, and volatility itself moves randomly over time.
    - **Continuous paths:** Real prices jump (earnings, events, gaps at the open), and delta hedging cannot fully remove jump risk.
    - **No transaction costs and continuous hedging:** Discrete hedging and costs leave residual P&L noise.

    This is why the market quotes in implied volatility rather than treating Black-Scholes as the true model.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Q7. How are theta and gamma related for a delta-hedged option?">
    The Black-Scholes PDE gives `Θ + r S Δ + (1/2) σ² S² Γ = r V`.

    For a delta-neutral position with small r, this is approximately `Θ ≈ −(1/2) σ² S² Γ`. A long-gamma holder pays theta every day and earns gamma profits when the stock moves. In the Black-Scholes world the two exactly offset at the implied volatility.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Q8. You are long a delta-hedged option. What is your daily P&L, and when do you make money?">
    Approximately: `P&L ≈ (1/2) Γ S² [ (ΔS / S)² − σ_implied² × dt ]`.

    You make money when the realized squared return exceeds the implied variance for that day, that is, when **realized volatility is higher than implied volatility**. Long gamma loses in the opposite case. This is the core of volatility trading: trading an option is essentially trading the gap between realized and implied volatility.

    This is an approximation. Vega P&L (the move in implied volatility itself) is separate, and how gamma is distributed across strikes and maturities affects the actual result.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Q9. Where is gamma largest? Where is vega largest? How do they change near expiry?">
    - **Gamma:** Largest at the money (ATM). Near expiry, ATM gamma rises sharply (pin risk) while in-the-money and out-of-the-money gamma goes to zero.
    - **Vega:** Largest near the money and increasing with time to expiry (vega is roughly proportional to √T). Vega goes to zero near expiry.
    - **Theta:** ATM options decay fastest close to expiry.

    So short-dated ATM options are high gamma and low vega, while long-dated options are low gamma and high vega.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## Volatility surface

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Q10. Why do equity index options show negative skew?">
    Downside strikes (puts) have higher implied volatility than upside strikes. Common explanations:

    - **Hedging demand:** Institutions buy puts to protect holdings, which pushes put prices up.
    - **Leverage effect:** When the stock falls, leverage rises and so does volatility, giving a negative correlation between price and volatility.
    - **Crash risk premium:** Investors pay a premium for downside tail protection.

    Skew shapes differ by underlying, and some single stocks have flatter or even upward-sloping skew.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Q11. What is the difference between sticky strike and sticky delta? Why does it matter for hedging?">
    - **Sticky strike:** When the underlying moves, the implied volatility at each fixed strike stays unchanged.
    - **Sticky delta (sticky moneyness):** Implied volatility moves with moneyness, so when the underlying rises, the implied volatility at a given strike changes with it.

    With skew, the two imply different effective deltas. Under sticky delta, a spot move also shifts implied volatility, so the effective delta equals the Black-Scholes delta plus `vega × (∂σ / ∂S)`. Your choice of assumption affects hedge ratios and P&L attribution.
  </Accordion>

  <Accordion title="Q12. How would you check a vol surface for arbitrage?">
    Two basic conditions:

    - **Calendar spread:** At a fixed strike, total variance `σ² × T` must increase with maturity, otherwise a calendar spread is an arbitrage.
    - **Butterfly:** At a fixed maturity, option price must be a convex function of strike, which means the implied risk-neutral density cannot be negative.

    Also check that call prices decrease in strike and that the call spread does not exceed the discounted strike difference. When fitting a surface (for example with an SVI-type parameterization), build these constraints into the fit.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>
